\begin{abstract}
This project demonstrates a workflow for fitting a finite-element-analysis (FEA) response model, calibrating it with a small number of observations, and testing predictions on withheld cases. The latest trial uses PyAnsys MAPDL to generate 80 training cases and 80 target cases for a straight, layered composite cantilever. Three model classes are compared: a mechanics-informed compliance regression, a quadratic response surface, and a Gaussian process. For each of four target sets, two responses calibrate carbon and glass stiffness scale factors; 18 other cases per repeat are held out for validation, with three calibration repeats. The models predict FEA-generated responses well after informative calibration for same-layup material changes. A deliberately uninformative calibration pair shows that two observations do not guarantee identifiable material corrections. The alternate-layup trial used configured material cards as its nominal baseline, so it demonstrates layup transfer with known nominal properties rather than prediction with no material knowledge. The study is FEA-to-FEA only; it does not validate physical material properties or a curved landing-gear leg. Large normalized deflections in some linear-static cases and outstanding mesh verification further limit physical interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Composite landing-gear design can require costly physical testing to determine how geometry, layup, and laminate stiffness affect deflection. This project investigates whether a model trained on FEA results can be corrected with only a few observations and used to predict unobserved responses. The long-term motivation is to apply the same workflow to physical test observations when they become available.

The present trial is a controlled methodology demonstration, not a landing-impact simulation or a mass optimization. It uses a straight cantilever as a tractable benchmark. No physical testing observations are included, and the material cards are numerical assumptions. Consequently, the validation performed here compares model predictions with held-out FEA results generated using the same modeling framework; it does not establish agreement with a manufactured gear.

\section{Finite-element model}
The beam is a flat rectangular layered shell with fixed length $L=0.25\,\mathrm{m}$. The axis is global $X$, width is global $Y$, and the applied transverse force is global $-Z$. The root edge is fully fixed. A total 10 N load is distributed equally across nodes on the free tip edge. The analysis is linear static.

The mesh uses SHELL181 layered shell elements, with 40 divisions along the length and 6 across the width. Three through-thickness integration points are assigned per ply. This is a two-dimensional shell discretization with a layered section, not a three-dimensional solid model. All cases use the same mesh.

Dataset A uses a symmetric twill/glass stack built by repeating carbon twill (T) and glass (G) in the half-stack and mirroring about the mid-plane. Carbon twill plies are 0.20 mm and glass plies are 0.10 mm. Dataset B's alternate-layup set uses a symmetric repeating carbon UD (U), carbon twill (T), and glass (G) half-stack, truncated to the needed half-ply count and mirrored; both carbon ply types are 0.20 mm and glass is 0.10 mm.

Orthotropic elastic and shear properties are specified in the example configuration. For the transfer experiment, a carbon factor scales the elastic and shear moduli of carbon plies, and a glass factor scales those of glass plies; density, Poisson ratios, and ply thickness do not change when these factors vary. The cards and cured ply values are computational assumptions, not measured project material properties.

The response is tip compliance,
\begin{equation}
 C=\frac{|\bar{u}_{z,\mathrm{tip}}|}{P},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{u}_{z,\mathrm{tip}}$ is the mean vertical tip-edge displacement and $P=10\,\mathrm{N}$. Compliance has units of m/N and is load-normalized for the linear-static analyses. The case files also record effective section properties, mass, ply sequence, thickness, signed deflection, and $|\delta|/L$.

\section{Experiment design and data}
\subsection{Dataset A: model training}
Dataset A contains the full 5 by 4 by 4 factorial design: five widths, four ply counts, and four carbon/glass property-factor combinations. The combinations are $(1,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$, and $(2,2)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Widths: 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.10 m.
\item Ply counts: 8, 16, 24, and 32.
\item Carbon/glass stiffness factor pairs: $(1,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$, and $(2,2)$.
\end{itemize}
This gives 80 FEA responses. The training cases span the base twill/glass stack and the configured carbon/glass stiffness variation.

\subsection{Dataset B: target sets}
Dataset B contains four 20-case sets (80 cases total). Except for geometry extrapolation, each set uses the Dataset A widths and ply counts.

The layup-transfer case is important to interpret correctly. Its FEA targets were generated with the material cards in the configuration at factors $(1,1)$. Those same configured cards and the known alternate ply sequence were used to calculate nominal laminate section properties for prediction. Thus nominal predictions were not made with no material knowledge: this set tests transfer to a different layup using known nominal material cards. During calibration, the model was allowed to adjust the carbon and glass factors around that baseline using only response observations. The configured truth factors were not supplied to the calibration optimizer.

\section{Models, calibration, and validation}
\subsection{Section-property predictors}
For each case, the effective laminate bending rigidity per unit width $\Dref$ is calculated from the laminate stiffness matrices. The transverse-shear rigidity per unit width is approximated as
\begin{equation}
 \Sref=\sum_k G_{xz,k}t_k.
\end{equation}
For a case of width $b$, the bending and corrected shear rigidities are $EI=b\Dref$ and $kGA=\kappa b\Sref$, with $\kappa=5/6$. The mechanical basis terms are
\begin{equation}
 x_b=\frac{L^3}{3EI}, \qquad x_s=\frac{L}{kGA}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Model families}
Three models are fit to Dataset A only:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mechanics-informed compliance regression:} nonnegative, no-intercept least squares in $x_b$ and $x_s$, $\widehat C=\beta_bx_b+\beta_sx_s$.
\item \textbf{Quadratic response surface:} second-order polynomial in standardized logarithms of width, total thickness, $\Dref$, and $\Sref$, fit to log compliance with ridge regularization.
\item \textbf{Gaussian process:} Mat\'ern 5/2 kernel plus a white-noise term, using the same log predictors and log compliance.
\end{enumerate}
Five-fold cross-validation on Dataset A estimates within-training-domain predictive performance. Target FEA responses are not used to fit or select the Dataset A models.

\subsection{Calibration and held-out validation}
For each target set, three calibration repeats are performed. A pair of target cases is randomly selected for each repeat; the same pair is used across model families. For each fitted model, nonlinear least squares estimates two multipliers, $q_C$ and $q_G$, by minimizing the squared log-compliance residuals at the selected cases:
\begin{equation}
 (q_C^*,q_G^*)=\arg\min_{q_C,q_G}\sum_{i\in\mathcal C}
 \left[\log\widehat C_i(q_C,q_G)-\log C_{i,\mathrm{FEA}}\right]^2,
 \qquad |\mathcal C|=2.
\end{equation}
Candidate factors are applied to the configured ply material cards, after which $\Dref$, $\Sref$, $EI$, and $kGA$ are recalculated. The trained model coefficients or surrogate surface are held fixed. The compliance observations are the calibration data; calculated section properties are model inputs, not target observations.

The other 18 cases are held out for validation in each repeat. The calibration cases do not appear in the validation metrics. Reported measures include MAPE, maximum absolute percentage error, RMSE, and $R^2$. Calibration fits two unknown factors from the algebraic minimum of two observations, so a poorly chosen pair may produce non-identifiable or unstable factors. Jacobian rank and condition number are recorded.

For the same-layup material-interpolation set, nominal predictions assume factors $(1,1)$ while the target uses $(1.5,1.5)$. The three-repeat average therefore hides a calibration-design failure; the fitted factors and conditioning must be examined alongside prediction scores.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The results show that a small number of observations can correct a response model when the target variation is represented by the calibration parameters and the selected observations are informative. They also show that low Dataset A cross-validation error does not guarantee robust target calibration. Calibration pair selection matters: two response measurements should produce distinct sensitivities to the unknown property factors. A high Jacobian condition number or deficient rank signals that the fitted factors should not be interpreted.

The alternate-layup and geometry-extrapolation cases had low nominal MAPE because their configured section properties were available and close to the training response domain. Calibration brought little benefit there and sometimes increased error by adjusting the property factors to absorb small model residuals. This is consistent with a correction method that should be applied only when justified by observations and diagnostics.

The analysis is linear static and uses 10 N. The maximum reported target $|\delta|/L$ is 1.70; 21 of 80 target cases and 22 of 80 training cases exceed 0.1. The most extreme target is the 8-ply, 0.015 m-wide case, with about 0.425 m tip deflection for a 0.25 m beam. These large-displacement cases are outside a small-deflection interpretation, even though the linear solver returns a compliance. Restrict the benchmark to a suitable small-deflection range or rerun at lower loads and state the intended validity domain.

All cases used a 40 by 6 mesh. The supplied result archive does not include a mesh-convergence study for these exact property-transfer cases, solver log, or MAPDL/PyMAPDL version record. Before interpreting sub-percent surrogate errors as meaningful FE accuracy, verify mesh sensitivity and reactions and check element orientation, supports, load application, and possible locking. The results do not address impact dynamics, failure, progressive damage, manufacturing constraints, or curved landing-gear geometry.

\section{Conclusion and next steps}
This trial implemented the planned training-calibration-validation workflow and compared three surrogate types. It provides a clear proof of concept for transferring compliance predictions between controlled FEA datasets when the material change can be represented by fitted stiffness scale factors. It also exposed two important qualifications: nominal predictions require a baseline material description, and calibration observations must be chosen to identify the unknown factors. The new-layup subset used known configured properties for its nominal predictions; it was not a no-material-knowledge test.

Next, select calibration pairs by maximizing sensitivity separation, add further repeats or calibration points to quantify robustness, and verify the FEA mesh and small-deflection domain. A subsequent study can then introduce observations from a trusted analytical model or physical testing. Applying the method to curved gear geometry and optimizing mass/stroke are future extensions, not outcomes of this trial.
